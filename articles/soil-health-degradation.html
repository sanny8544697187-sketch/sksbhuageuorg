Soil Health and Soil Degradation: Causes, Effects and Management

📅 August 2025  ·  ⏱ 9 min read  ·  🎓 BHU Agriculture Students
← Back to All Articles

Soil is arguably the most important natural resource for agriculture. The health of our soil directly determines the health of our food, our farmers, and our nation. Yet India loses thousands of tonnes of fertile topsoil every year to erosion, waterlogging, salinity, and chemical degradation. Understanding soil health and its threats is not only crucial for exams but for any agriculture student who wants to make a real difference in Indian farming.

What is Soil Health?

The USDA (United States Department of Agriculture) defines soil health as: "The continued capacity of soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains plants, animals, and humans."

Soil health is often used interchangeably with soil quality, though some researchers distinguish them — soil quality is more about physical and chemical properties, while soil health emphasizes the biological and dynamic nature of soil as a living system.

Indicators of Soil Health

A) Physical Indicators

  • Soil texture: Relative proportion of sand, silt, and clay
  • Soil structure: Arrangement of soil particles into aggregates
  • Bulk density: Mass of soil per unit volume (lower = healthier, better aeration)
  • Water holding capacity (WHC): Amount of water soil can retain against gravity
  • Infiltration rate: Speed at which water enters the soil

B) Chemical Indicators

  • Soil pH: Optimal range for most crops: 6.0–7.5
  • Soil organic carbon (SOC): Most important indicator. India's average SOC is low (0.3–0.5%) vs global average of 1.5–2%
  • Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC): Soil's ability to hold cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, H⁺)
  • Available NPK: Plant-available nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium levels
  • Electrical Conductivity (EC): Measure of salt content in soil

C) Biological Indicators

  • Microbial biomass carbon (MBC): Total mass of living microorganisms in soil
  • Soil respiration rate: CO₂ released by microbial activity — indicates organic matter decomposition
  • Earthworm population: "Ecosystem engineers" — 100+ earthworms/m² indicates healthy soil
  • Dehydrogenase enzyme activity: Indicates overall microbial metabolic activity

💡 Remember: Soil Health Card Scheme

The Government of India's Soil Health Card (SHC) Scheme (launched 2015) tests soil for 12 parameters: N, P, K, S, Zn, Fe, Mn, Cu, B, pH, EC, and OC. Target: test every farm every 2 years. Over 22 crore SHCs distributed so far.

Soil Degradation: Types and Definition

Soil degradation is defined as the decline in soil quality as measured by changes in soil properties and processes, and the consequent reduction in soil's capacity to support biodiversity and maintain environmental quality. It is one of India's most serious environmental challenges, affecting about 120.72 million hectares of land.

1. Water Erosion

The most widespread form of soil degradation in India. Rainfall dislodges soil particles; runoff transports them away. India loses approximately 5,334 million tonnes of soil annually to erosion. Major types: Sheet erosion, Rill erosion, Gully erosion, Stream bank erosion. Most common in: Deccan Plateau, Eastern Ghats, Shivalik Hills.

2. Wind Erosion

Predominant in arid and semi-arid regions — Rajasthan, Gujarat, Haryana. Fine soil particles are carried by wind, forming sand dunes. Reduces soil fertility and creates desert-like conditions (desertification).

3. Waterlogging

Excess water in the root zone that saturates the soil, cutting off oxygen to roots. Causes: over-irrigation, poor drainage, high water table. Affects about 8.53 million ha in India. Severely impacts states like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal.

4. Soil Salinity and Alkalinity

  • Saline soils: EC > 4 dS/m, pH < 8.5. Caused by: evaporation of saline groundwater, seawater intrusion, excessive use of poor-quality irrigation water.
  • Alkali/Sodic soils (USAR): High exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP > 15), pH > 8.5. Common in Indo-Gangetic plains. India has about 6.73 million ha of salt-affected soils.

5. Soil Compaction

Heavy machinery (tractors, combines) causes compaction of the plough layer and even sub-surface. Increases bulk density, reduces pore space, root penetration, and water infiltration. Common problem of mechanized intensive farming in Punjab, Haryana.

6. Chemical Degradation

Excessive use of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides reduces soil microbial diversity, acidifies soil (urea hydrolysis), and creates nutrient imbalances. Long-term monoculture depletes specific nutrients and breaks down soil structure.

Effects of Soil Degradation on Crop Productivity

  • Reduced availability of plant nutrients — lower crop yields
  • Decreased water holding capacity — more irrigation needed
  • Increased susceptibility to drought stress
  • Loss of soil biodiversity — reduced natural pest and disease suppression
  • Economic losses to farmers — higher input costs, lower returns
  • Food security threat — India cannot afford to lose productive farmland

Management and Reclamation of Degraded Soils

A) Conservation Tillage

Minimum disturbance to soil — zero-tillage or reduced tillage. Retains crop residues as mulch. Reduces erosion, improves soil organic matter. Zero-till wheat sowing (Happy Seeder) in Punjab-Haryana is a major success story.

B) Cover Crops and Green Manure

Growing legumes (cowpea, dhaincha) between main crop seasons covers bare soil, prevents erosion, adds organic matter and nitrogen. Vetiver grass (Khus) planted along contours prevents severe erosion on slopes.

C) Soil and Water Conservation Structures

  • Contour bunding and terracing — reduces runoff velocity on slopes
  • Check dams and gully plugs — traps sediment and recharges groundwater
  • Watershed management — integrated approach at catchment level

D) Reclamation of Salt-Affected Soils

  • Saline soils: Leaching with excess water; improving drainage; growing salt-tolerant crops (barley, Karnal grass)
  • Alkali/Sodic soils: Application of Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) @ 5-10 t/ha to replace Na⁺ with Ca²⁺; green manuring; growing Sesbania and rice in reclamation phase

Government Initiatives for Soil Conservation

  • National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA): Umbrella mission under NAPCC. Covers soil health management, rainfed area development, and climate change adaptation.
  • Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY): Promotes efficient irrigation to prevent waterlogging and salinity.
  • Integrated Watershed Management Programme (IWMP): Watershed-based soil and water conservation across 39 million ha.
  • Soil Health Card Scheme: Nationwide soil testing and advisory to prevent chemical degradation from improper fertilizer use.

🎯 Key Exam Points — Soil Health and Degradation

  • India's salt-affected soils: ~6.73 million ha
  • Annual soil loss to erosion: 5,334 million tonnes/year
  • Saline soil: EC > 4 dS/m | Alkali soil: pH > 8.5, ESP > 15
  • Gypsum used for reclamation of sodic/alkali soils
  • India's average SOC: 0.3–0.5% (very low)
  • SHC Scheme tests 12 parameters; launched 2015
  • Earthworms — "ecosystem engineers" / "farmer's best friend"
  • Bulk density of healthy soil: 1.0–1.3 g/cm³ for loam

Conclusion

Soil health is the foundation of sustainable agriculture. With over 120 million hectares of degraded land in India, soil conservation is not just an environmental issue but an economic and food security imperative. For agriculture students, understanding soil degradation types, their causes, effects, and management strategies — from gypsum application to watershed management — is critical for both examinations and for contributing to the future of Indian farming.
