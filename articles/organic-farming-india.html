Organic Farming in India: Principles, Benefits and Scope

📅 August 2025  ·  ⏱ 8 min read  ·  🎓 BHU Agriculture Students
← Back to All Articles

Organic farming is one of the most rapidly growing and discussed topics in Indian agriculture. As concerns over food safety, soil degradation, and environmental sustainability mount, organic farming has emerged not just as a buzzword but as a viable, scientifically-backed agricultural system. For agriculture students — especially those preparing for ICAR JRF, BHU exams, and UPSC AF — a thorough understanding of organic farming principles, practices, and policy framework is essential.

What is Organic Farming?

Organic farming is a holistic production management system that avoids the use of synthetic fertilizers, synthetic pesticides, growth regulators, and genetically modified organisms (GMOs). Instead, it relies on ecological processes, biodiversity, and cycles adapted to local conditions to maintain soil fertility, crop health, and ecosystem balance.

The International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM) defines organic agriculture as: "A production system that sustains the health of soils, ecosystems and people."

Four Principles of Organic Farming (IFOAM)

IFOAM recognizes four fundamental principles that guide the practice and development of organic agriculture:

  1. Principle of Health: Organic agriculture should sustain and enhance the health of soil, plant, animal, human, and planet as one and indivisible.
  2. Principle of Ecology: Organic agriculture should be based on living ecological systems and cycles, work with them, emulate them, and help sustain them.
  3. Principle of Fairness: Organic agriculture should build on relationships that ensure fairness with regard to the common environment and life opportunities.
  4. Principle of Care: Organic agriculture should be managed in a precautionary and responsible manner to protect the health and wellbeing of current and future generations.

Organic Inputs Commonly Used in India

A) Organic Manures and Fertilizers

  • Farm Yard Manure (FYM): Decomposed mixture of dung, urine, and crop residues. NPK content approximately 0.5:0.25:0.5%.
  • Vermicompost: Prepared using earthworms (Eisenia fetida, Lumbricus rubellus). Rich in NPK, growth hormones, and beneficial microbes. Used at 2-4 tonnes/ha.
  • Compost: Decomposed organic waste (crop residues, kitchen waste). Improves soil structure and water holding capacity.
  • Green Manure Crops: Dhaincha (Sesbania aculeata), Sunhemp (Crotalaria juncea), Cowpea. Incorporated before flowering. Adds 80-120 kg N/ha.

B) Bio-fertilizers

  • Rhizobium: Symbiotic N-fixation in legumes. Can fix 50-300 kg N/ha/year.
  • Azospirillum: Associative symbiosis with cereals (wheat, rice, maize). Fixes 20-40 kg N/ha.
  • Azotobacter: Free-living N-fixer. Used for vegetable crops.
  • PSB (Phosphate Solubilizing Bacteria): Bacillus and Pseudomonas species. Solubilize insoluble P for plant uptake.
  • Mycorrhiza (VAM): Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhiza enhances P, Zn, Cu uptake and drought tolerance.

💡 Exam Tip: Important Quantities

FYM application: 10-15 tonnes/ha | Vermicompost: 2-4 tonnes/ha | Green manure N addition: 80-120 kg N/ha. These numbers are frequently asked in objective questions.

Benefits of Organic Farming

1. Soil Health Benefits

  • Improves soil organic carbon (SOC) content and microbial biomass
  • Enhances soil water holding capacity and aeration
  • Reduces soil compaction and prevents erosion
  • Promotes beneficial soil organisms (earthworms, microbes)

2. Environmental Benefits

  • Reduces groundwater contamination from chemical runoff
  • Lowers greenhouse gas emissions (no synthetic N fertilizers)
  • Promotes biodiversity — beneficial insects, birds, and soil organisms
  • Prevents long-term land degradation

3. Economic Benefits for Farmers

  • Premium market price: organic produce fetches 20-50% higher price
  • Reduced input costs after transition period (3 years)
  • Access to export markets (EU, USA, Japan organic demand)
  • India is the world's 9th largest organic producer (FIBL data)

Challenges of Organic Farming in India

Despite its benefits, organic farming faces several challenges in India:

  • Yield gap during transition: Yields typically drop 10-30% in the first 3 years before recovering.
  • Certification cost: Third-party organic certification (PGS or NPOP) is expensive and complex for small farmers.
  • Limited bio-input availability: Quality vermicompost, bio-fertilizers, and bio-pesticides are not readily available in all regions.
  • Market linkage: Farmers often lack direct market access and are exploited by middlemen.
  • Knowledge gap: Inadequate training and extension support for organic transition.

Government Schemes for Organic Farming

Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY)

Launched in 2015 under Soil Health Management Scheme (NMSA). Key features:

  • Cluster-based approach — groups of 50 farmers covering 50 acres per cluster
  • Financial assistance: ₹50,000/ha over 3 years (₹31,000/ha to farmers)
  • Covers PGS (Participatory Guarantee System) certification cost
  • Promotes indigenous organic inputs (Beejamrit, Jeevamrit, panchagavya)

Mission Organic Value Chain Development (MOVCDNER)

Specifically for North-East India. Supports organic value chain from input production to marketing. Provides ₹46,000/ha financial support over 3 years.

Organic Certification in India

India has two systems for organic certification:

  • NPOP (National Programme for Organic Production): For export purposes. Managed by APEDA. Equivalent to EU and USA organic standards.
  • PGS-India (Participatory Guarantee System): For domestic markets. Farmer-managed quality assurance. Managed by PKVY clusters.

🎯 Key Exam Points — Organic Farming

  • India's organic area: approx. 4.19 million ha (2022) — Sikkim is 100% organic state
  • First 100% organic state in the world: Sikkim (certified in 2016)
  • IFOAM four principles: Health, Ecology, Fairness, Care
  • Transition period for organic certification: 3 years
  • Bio-fertilizer Rhizobium is specific to legumes — species-specific inoculation needed
  • VAM (Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhiza) most effective for P uptake in poor soils
  • PKVY launched in 2015 — ₹50,000/ha support over 3 years

Conclusion

Organic farming represents a paradigm shift from the chemical-intensive Green Revolution model towards a more sustainable, ecology-driven agricultural system. While challenges like yield gap during transition and market linkage remain, the increasing global and domestic demand for organic produce, coupled with strong government support through schemes like PKVY, makes organic farming a promising and growing sector in Indian agriculture.

For agriculture students, understanding organic farming — from IFOAM principles to bio-fertilizer mechanisms to government policies — is not just exam preparation but also preparation for contributing to a more sustainable agricultural future for India.
